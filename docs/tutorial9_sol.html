Tutorial 9: (Women) labour force participation

In this exercise, we are going to learn:

  1. How households decide on their labour supply
  2. How the family size affect the latter
  3. Estimate the effects of having children on women labour participation

Theoretical approach of labour supply

Consider an individual who derives her utility from consuming a good \(c\) and leisure \(l\). The good comes at price \(p=1\), it is the numéraire. Utility function is:

\[ U(c,l) = c^\alpha \times l^{1-\alpha} \]

The individual earns a labour wage \(w\). The number of hours worked is denoted \(h\), hence the labour income is \(wh\). She also obtains a lump-sum benefit from the government \(y\). Hence, her total income is \(wh + y\). There is a fixed number of hours \(T\) in each day, such that \(T = l+h\).

  1. What are the two constraints? Can you express the budget constraint as a function of leisure \(l\), consumption, and total amount of time \(T\) only?
  2. Write down the maximization problem and the associated Lagrangian.
  3. Solve the maximization program to find \(c^\star\) and \(h^\star\).

We are now going to proceed to some comparative statics, i.e. we are going to twist some parameters to understand the partial equilibrium effects.

  1. How does \(h^\star\) evolve with wage? With the lump-sum benefit?

Now, we are going to assume that the individual has a positive number of children \(n>0 \in \mathbb{N}\). Then, she must allocate a fixed number of hours to take care of their children. Each child requires \(K\) hours of childcare, hence the total number of hours is \(nK\). In practice, it reduces the number of hours available to leisure and work.

  1. How does it affect labour supply? Is there a threshold upon which the mother stops providing labour?
Solution

Q1.

The individual faces two constraints:

  1. Budget constraint: consumption equals total income: \(c = wh + y\).
  2. Time constraint: total time is split between work and leisure: \(h + l = T\), so \(h = T - l\).

Substituting the time constraint into the budget constraint eliminates \(h\):

\[c + wl = wT + y\]

This is the full-income budget constraint: the price of leisure is the opportunity cost of not working, i.e. the wage \(w\), and total “full income” is \(wT + y\).

Q2.

\[\max_{c,\, l} \; c^\alpha l^{1-\alpha} \quad \text{subject to} \quad c + wl = wT + y, \quad c, l \geq 0\]

The Lagrangian is:

\[\mathcal{L}(c, l, \lambda) = c^\alpha l^{1-\alpha} - \lambda\bigl(c + wl - wT - y\bigr)\]

Q3.

Setting the partial derivatives to zero:

\[\frac{\partial \mathcal{L}}{\partial c} = \alpha c^{\alpha-1} l^{1-\alpha} - \lambda = 0\] \[\frac{\partial \mathcal{L}}{\partial l} = (1-\alpha) c^\alpha l^{-\alpha} - \lambda w = 0\] \[\frac{\partial \mathcal{L}}{\partial \lambda} = -(c + wl - wT - y) = 0\]

Dividing the first FOC by the second:

\[\frac{\alpha l}{(1-\alpha) c} = \frac{1}{w} \implies c = \frac{\alpha w l}{1-\alpha}\]

Substituting into the budget constraint:

\[\frac{\alpha w l}{1-\alpha} + wl = wT + y \implies wl \cdot \frac{1}{1-\alpha} = wT + y\]

\[\boxed{l^\star = (1-\alpha)\!\left(T + \frac{y}{w}\right), \qquad c^\star = \alpha(wT + y)}\]

Since \(h^\star = T - l^\star\):

\[\boxed{h^\star = \alpha T - (1-\alpha)\frac{y}{w}}\]

The individual allocates fraction \(\alpha\) of her “full income” to consumption and fraction \(1-\alpha\) to leisure — a hallmark of Cobb-Douglas preferences.

Q4.

\[\frac{\partial h^\star}{\partial w} = \frac{(1-\alpha)\, y}{w^2} \geq 0\]

Higher wages increase labour supply (or leave it unchanged when \(y = 0\), because the income and substitution effects cancel each other).

\[\frac{\partial h^\star}{\partial y} = -\frac{1-\alpha}{w} < 0\]

A higher lump-sum benefit reduces labour supply: with more unearned income, the individual can afford more leisure (pure income effect — there is no change in the relative price of leisure).

Q5.

With \(n\) children each requiring \(K\) hours of childcare, the time constraint becomes:

\[h + l + nK = T \implies h + l = T - nK\]

The analysis is identical to before, with \(T\) replaced by \(T - nK\):

\[h^\star = \alpha(T - nK) - (1-\alpha)\frac{y}{w}\]

Labour supply decreases by \(\alpha K\) for each additional child.

The mother exits the labour market when \(h^\star \leq 0\):

\[\alpha(T - nK) \leq (1-\alpha)\frac{y}{w} \implies n \geq n^\star \equiv \frac{\alpha w T - (1-\alpha)y}{\alpha w K}\]

The threshold \(n^\star\) is lower when the wage is lower (working is less attractive), when unearned income \(y\) is higher (the income effect makes labour less necessary), and when childcare cost \(K\) is higher.

Empirical approach

The theoretical prediction is that mothers might drop out the labour force because they have children. We are going to estimate this effect.

  1. Write a model with the labor force participation on the left hand side: we want to identify the effect of being the mother (of a large family) on labor force participation.
  2. Create the relevant variables.

  4. Is there endogeneity? Which variables would you include to tackle it?
  5. Add household fixed effects. Why part of endogeneity do they tackle?
  6. Is there any endogeneity left?
  7. A first potential instrument for large families is the presence of twins among the second born children. Why? Discuss the validity of this instrument.

Solution

Q2.

The model is a linear probability model (LPM) estimated on the full sample (men and women):

\[\text{LFP}_i = \beta_0 + \beta_1 \,\text{woman}_i + \beta_2\, \text{child}_i + \beta_3\,(\text{woman}_i \times \text{child}_i) + \beta_4\, \text{large}_i + \beta_5\,(\text{woman}_i \times \text{large}_i) + \varepsilon_i\]

where:

  • \(\text{woman}_i = \mathbf{1}[\text{nsexe}_i = 2]\)
  • \(\text{child}_i = \mathbf{1}[\text{mne1}_i \geq 1]\) (applies to both spouses)
  • \(\text{large}_i = \mathbf{1}[\text{mne1}_i > 2]\) (applies to both spouses)
  • \(\text{woman}_i \times \text{child}_i\): the individual is a mother
  • \(\text{woman}_i \times \text{large}_i\): the individual is a mother in a large family

Q3. See R code.

Q4.

  • \(\beta_1 < 0\): women have lower LFP than men even without children (baseline gender gap).
  • \(\beta_2 > 0\): having a child increases LFP for men — fathers work more to provide.
  • \(\beta_3 \approx 0\): having 1–2 children imposes no additional penalty on women relative to fathers, once the baseline gender gap and fatherhood effect are accounted for.
  • \(\beta_4 < 0\): a large family slightly reduces LFP for fathers too.
  • \(\beta_5 < 0\): mothers in large families face a large additional penalty — this is the main channel through which children reduce women’s LFP.

Q5.

Yes, there is likely omitted variable bias (OVB). Individuals in larger families may differ systematically in ways that also affect LFP:

  • Age (nag): older individuals may have both more children and different LFP patterns.
  • Education (ndiplo): more educated women tend to have fewer children and higher LFP.

Including nag and ndiplo as controls reduces OVB from observable confounders.

Q6.

Household fixed effects absorb all time-invariant, household-level unobservables — the couple’s preferences, local labour market conditions, cultural norms, the partner’s income, etc. Within a household, child and large are the same for both spouses and are therefore absorbed by the FE. However, woman, woman × child, and woman × large do vary within the household (wife vs. husband). The HH FE estimator identifies these coefficients by comparing the wife’s and husband’s LFP within the same household, netting out all shared household-level confounders.

Q7.

Yes. Both child and large are endogenous: fertility is a choice jointly determined with labour supply. This means woman × child and woman × large are endogenous too, since they embed the endogenous fertility variables. OLS — even with household FE — will produce biased estimates because unobserved preferences for work vs. family are correlated with both fertility decisions and LFP.

Q8.

Twins are a valid instrument for having a large family because:

  • Relevance: a twin birth mechanically increases family size by one, raising the probability of having more than 2 children.
  • Exclusion restriction: the occurrence of a twin birth is essentially random (at least conditional on IVF usage and mother’s age) and does not directly affect labour supply other than through the increased family size.

However, large and woman × large are both endogenous — two instruments are needed. The standard solution (Angrist & Evans 1998) is to restrict the sample to households with at least 2 children: in this sub-sample child = 1 for everyone, so it drops out as a constant and is no longer endogenous. Two endogenous variables remain: large and woman × large. Interacting the instrument with woman (woman × twin) provides the second instrument — giving a just-identified system.

Q9. See R code. The LATE recovered is the effect of a large family on individuals in households that had a twin second birth. A first-stage F well above 10 for both endogenous variables confirms instrument relevance.

Q10.

When the first two children are of the same gender, parents are more likely to want a third child (preference for at least one child of each gender is widespread). Thus same_sexe predicts having more than 2 children.

  • Relevance: same-gender sibship raises the probability of a third child.
  • Exclusion restriction: the gender composition of children is determined at conception and is independent of the mother’s labour supply intentions. Conditional on having at least 2 children, the composition of their genders is exogenous. Note however that same-gender children may reduce housing costs (families with mixed-gender children are more likely to seek an extra bedroom), which could be an additional channel — a potential violation of the exclusion restriction.

As for the twin instrument, same_sexe is interacted with woman to provide the second instrument for woman × large.

Q11. See R code. Estimates from the same-gender instrument identify a different LATE (compliers are parents who had a third child because of same-gender preference). Comparing the two IV estimates provides a robustness check on instrument validity.
